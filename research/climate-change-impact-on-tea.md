# Climate Change Impact on Tea

**Research Note** | Comparative crop for commodity climate risk analysis

## Summary

Tea (*Camellia sinensis*) is highly sensitive to temperature extremes, rainfall patterns, and humidity. Climate change is already altering yields, quality, and the geographic suitability of major producing regions (China, India, Kenya, Sri Lanka). Impacts are regionally heterogeneous: some cooler or higher-altitude areas may see temporary gains, while traditional heartlands face increasing stress.

---

## 1. Key Climate Sensitivities

| Factor | Effect on Tea |
|--------|---------------|
| **Temperature** | Optimal roughly 18–30 °C range depending on region/cultivar. Monthly averages above ~26.6 °C in Assam linked to yield declines (~3.8% per additional 1 °C at higher baselines). Both heat and cold extremes reduce yields. |
| **Heat extremes** | Days >34–35 °C associated with significant yield losses (e.g. ~3.7% per extreme heat day in China studies). |
| **Cold extremes / frost** | Still damaging in some regions; each cold day (0–4 °C) can cut yield 2–4%. Warming may reduce cold stress in northern areas. |
| **Rainfall / drought** | Critical for flush cycles. Reduced or poorly timed rainfall lowers yields; drought stress intensifies with higher VPD. Excess rain or high humidity can increase disease pressure. |
| **CO₂ fertilization** | Can partially offset temperature stress in some models, especially under higher emission scenarios. |

Quality (biochemical composition, flavour compounds) is also affected by temperature and seasonal shifts — often more complex and variable than pure yield effects.

---

## 2. Regional Outlooks

**China** (largest producer)  
- Currently more limited by cold extremes in some areas.  
- Future: reduced cold stress but emerging heat-stress losses (projected 11–26% heat-induced losses in key provinces under 1.5–2 °C warming).  
- Net yield impacts mixed; some models show increases under stronger CO₂ effects.

**India (esp. Assam & Northeast)**  
- Rising Tmax already observed.  
- Yields decline when monthly temperatures exceed ~26.6 °C.  
- Projections of significant production risk by 2050 without adaptation; suitability changes complex with possible upslope shifts.

**Kenya**  
- High vulnerability. Optimal suitability projected to decline substantially (studies cite ~26% loss of optimal areas by 2050 in some models).  
- Heat + water stress expected to reduce yields (e.g. ~5–10% under mid-century scenarios depending on soil moisture changes).

**Sri Lanka**  
- Optimal suitability declines of ~14% by 2050 and higher by 2070 in multiple projections.  
- Sensitive to both temperature and rainfall variability.

**Other**  
- Some African producers (Malawi, Tanzania, Argentina) face large projected losses of highly suitable land (60%+ in some studies).  
- Turkey and certain higher-latitude or higher-altitude zones may see suitability gains.

Overall, more than half of the top 20 tea-producing countries are projected to lose highly suitable area by 2050 under both moderate and high-emission scenarios. Marginally suitable areas may expand, implying geographic shifts.

---

## 3. Comparison with Coffee

| Aspect | Coffee (esp. Arabica) | Tea |
|--------|-----------------------|-----|
| Temperature sensitivity | Very high, narrow optimum | High, somewhat broader |
| Frost risk | Critical in Brazil | Relevant but secondary in many regions |
| Drought | Major yield driver | Important, interacts with heat |
| Quality impact | Significant (bean size, cup) | Very significant (chemical profile, flavour) |
| Geographic shift potential | Upslope / higher latitude | Similar upslope potential |
| CO₂ effect | Partial offset | Often stronger positive offset in models |

Both crops face rising climate risk, but tea’s perennial nature and continuous harvesting (flushes) create different seasonal vulnerability patterns than coffee’s annual crop cycle.

---

## 4. Implications for Commodity Research

- Climate-driven supply shocks in tea are less frequently the dominant price driver than in coffee (tea market structure and stock dynamics differ), but extreme events still matter.
- Quality degradation can affect premiums for specialty / orthodox teas more than bulk black tea.
- Monitoring temperature extremes, soil moisture, and seasonal rainfall in Kenya, Assam, and Sri Lanka offers potential early signals.
- Cross-crop analysis (coffee + tea) can reveal broader beverage-crop climate risk patterns.
